# What Is Qi?

To understand Shaolin's internal arts, we must meet a word that has no exact English translation: **Qi** (氣, also written *chi* or *ki*). It is usually rendered as "vital energy" or "life force," but it is better understood through what it *does* than through any single definition.

## Breath, Energy, and Life

The character for Qi originally depicted **vapour rising from cooking rice** — breath, steam, the invisible something that animates the living. In Chinese thought, Qi is the energy that flows through all things: through the body along channels called **meridians**, through the seasons, through the landscape. Where Qi flows freely, there is health and vitality. Where it stagnates or is depleted, there is illness and fatigue.

You do not have to adopt the full traditional cosmology to find the idea useful. Treat Qi, if you like, as a vivid and practical *language* for things you can genuinely feel: the warmth that spreads through the body after deep breathing, the sense of rootedness in a strong stance, the calm alertness of a settled mind. **Qi Gong** (氣功) means, simply, "the work or cultivation of Qi" — the disciplined practice of building and guiding this energy.

## The Three Treasures

Classical practice speaks of **three treasures** (*San Bao*) that the practitioner cultivates:

- **Jing** (精) — *essence*. The deep, foundational vitality, associated with the body, the kidneys, and our inherited constitution. Jing is precious and spent slowly; it is conserved through rest, moderation, and gentle practice.
- **Qi** (氣) — *energy*. The functional, circulating force that powers daily life and movement. Qi is replenished through breath, food, and exercise.
- **Shen** (神) — *spirit* or *mind*. Awareness, presence, the light in the eyes. Shen is cultivated through meditation and a settled heart.

The aim of internal practice is to **conserve Jing, build Qi, and brighten Shen** — and to keep all three in harmony.

## The Dan Tian

Central to Qi Gong is the **Dan Tian** (丹田), the "elixir field," a point and region about **two to three finger-widths below the navel and deep within the lower abdomen**. It is regarded as the body's reservoir and furnace of Qi — the place from which rooted power and steady breath arise.

This is why nearly every Shaolin breathing exercise asks you to breathe *low*, into the belly: you are filling and warming the Dan Tian. When teachers say "sink the Qi to the Dan Tian," they mean something you can practise right now — let the breath drop, let the shoulders relax, let your centre of gravity and your attention settle below the navel.

## The Foundation of Everything

Qi Gong is not separate from the breathing patterns and stances elsewhere in this app — it is their inner dimension. Box breathing fills the Dan Tian; the Horse Stance roots the Qi; the Eight Pieces of Brocade circulate it through the whole body.

In the chapters that follow we will meet two of Shaolin's great Qi Gong treasures — the **Eight Pieces of Brocade** and the **Five Animals** — and finally the legendary **Muscle-Tendon Classic** and the art of **standing like a tree**. But all of them rest on this first quiet skill: to breathe low, settle the mind, and feel the body come alive.
