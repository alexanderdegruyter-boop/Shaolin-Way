# The Muscle-Tendon Classic & Standing Like a Tree

Two practices sit at the deep heart of Shaolin's internal training. One is a famous classical method, the **Yijin Jing**; the other is the deceptively simple art of **standing still**. Both build the kind of strength that does not show — the strength of tendon, breath, and rooted calm.

## The Yijin Jing — Changing the Muscles and Tendons

The **Yijin Jing** (易筋經), the "Muscle-Tendon Change Classic," is a set of exercises traditionally — though, as we saw in the history, only legendarily — attributed to Bodhidharma. Its purpose is right there in the name: to **transform and strengthen the muscles, and especially the tendons and connective tissue**, turning a weak or stiff body into a strong, supple, and resilient one.

Where the Eight Pieces of Brocade are gentle and flowing, the Yijin Jing introduces **isometric effort and dynamic tension**: postures held with the muscles deliberately engaged, the breath steady, the mind focused on the working part of the body. The classic teaches that lasting power lives not in bulky muscle but in **strong, elastic tendons** — the springs of the body. This is why traditional Shaolin training prizes slow, held, tension-filled work alongside fast movement.

The core principles you can apply today:

- **Engage and hold.** Strength is built in postures held with intention, not only in repetitions.
- **Breathe through effort.** Never hold the breath against tension; let slow breath carry you through.
- **Direct the mind.** Place your attention exactly where the work is. The mind leads the Qi, and the Qi leads the strength.

## Zhan Zhuang — Standing Like a Tree

Perhaps the most fundamental internal exercise of all is **Zhan Zhuang** (站桩), "standing like a post" or "standing like a tree." You simply **stand**, relaxed yet structured, for minutes at a time — and discover that nothing is simple about it.

A basic standing posture:

1. Feet shoulder-width, parallel, knees softly bent.
2. The spine long, as if a thread lifts the crown of the head; the chin slightly tucked.
3. The arms rounded in front of the chest as though gently holding a large, light ball.
4. The shoulders dropped, the chest relaxed, the breath sinking to the Dan Tian.
5. Hold. Breathe. Soften wherever you find tension. Let the mind grow quiet and the body grow rooted.

Begin with two or three minutes and build slowly toward ten or twenty. At first the arms burn and the mind rebels; in time a deep, settled strength and calm arise. Zhan Zhuang trains the tendons, the breath, the rooting, and the mind all at once — which is why generations of masters have called it the foundation beneath every other skill.

## The Inner Work

The Yijin Jing and Zhan Zhuang share a secret that the whole of Shaolin's internal art turns upon: **the most powerful work is often the most still.** No leaping, no spectacle — just a held posture, a slow breath, and a focused mind, repeated patiently until the body is remade from the inside.

Bring this spirit to everything in this app. When you hold the Horse Stance, you are doing Yijin Jing. When you breathe into the Dan Tian, you are gathering Qi. When you stand quiet before practice, you are standing like a tree. The temple may be far away on its mountain — but its deepest training fits in the small, still space where you stand right now.
