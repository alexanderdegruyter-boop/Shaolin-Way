# Origins of the Way

> "Before enlightenment, chop wood, carry water. After enlightenment, chop wood, carry water."

High in the wooded Song Mountains of Henan province stands a monastery whose name has travelled across the world: **Shaolin**, the "young forest." It is here, more than fifteen centuries ago, that a quiet meeting of two great rivers took place — the contemplative stillness of Indian **Chan** (later known in Japan as Zen) and the disciplined movement of Chinese martial tradition.

## A Monk and a Wall

The story is told of **Bodhidharma**, an Indian monk who travelled to China and, finding the monks of Shaolin weak from long hours of seated meditation, taught them simple exercises to strengthen the body. He is said to have sat facing a cave wall for nine years in silent meditation. Whether legend or history, the lesson endures: the mind cannot be cultivated in a body that has been neglected, and the body cannot be mastered by one whose mind is scattered.

This is the first principle of the Shaolin way. **Body and mind are not two things to be trained separately, but one thing to be cultivated together.**

## Why the Monks Trained

The monks did not learn to move in order to fight. They learned to move in order to *sit* — to hold the body still and strong through long meditation, to endure cold and hunger, and to keep the temple and its travellers safe. Martial skill was a by-product of health, discipline, and presence, never the goal itself.

When we train today — a stance held until the legs tremble, a breath drawn slowly into the belly, a chapter read by lamplight — we are joining a stream that has flowed for fifteen hundred years. We do not train to become hard. We train to become *whole*.

## What This Book Offers

The chapters that follow are not a history lesson. They are an invitation to a way of living:

- To meet each day with a **clear and quiet mind**.
- To build a **strong and supple body** through patient practice.
- To treat **discipline not as punishment** but as a form of self-respect.

You need no temple and no master to begin. You need only a small space, a few quiet minutes, and the willingness to return tomorrow.

Turn the page, and let us begin where the monks began — with the mind.
