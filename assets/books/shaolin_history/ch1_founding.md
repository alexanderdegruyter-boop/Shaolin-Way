# The Founding of the Temple

The Shaolin Monastery sits on the forested slopes of **Mount Song** (Songshan), one of the Five Sacred Mountains of China, in Henan province. Its name, *Shàolín* (少林), means "young forest" or "forest of Mount Shaoshi" — Shaoshi being the western peak of the Song range at whose foot the temple was built.

## A Temple for a Travelling Monk

The monastery was founded in **495 CE**, during the **Northern Wei dynasty**, by order of **Emperor Xiaowen**. He built it for an Indian Buddhist master named **Batuo** (also known by the Sanskrit name *Buddhabhadra*), who had travelled to China to teach. Batuo was not a martial artist; he was a translator and teacher of Buddhist scripture, and the early Shaolin was a place of study and translation, not fighting.

For its first decades, Shaolin was simply one of many imperial monasteries — a quiet centre of Buddhist scholarship, where monks copied and translated sutras brought along the Silk Road from India.

## Why the Mountains

The choice of Mount Song was deliberate. In Chinese tradition the sacred mountains were thresholds between the human and the divine, places where the air was clean, the world was quiet, and the mind could settle. A monastery in such a place was protected by distance from the noise of cities and the intrigues of court.

But mountains are also wild. The forests around Shaolin held bandits and beasts, and the monks — who travelled, who guarded the temple's relics and grain, and who sat in long, body-punishing meditation — had practical reasons to keep themselves strong and able to defend what they had. The seeds of Shaolin's later martial fame were planted not in aggression but in the simple needs of a remote mountain community.

## The Stage Is Set

So at its founding, Shaolin held the three things from which everything later would grow:

- A tradition of **deep Buddhist study and meditation**.
- A **remote, demanding mountain home** that asked for hardiness.
- A community of monks who understood that **a disciplined body served a disciplined mind**.

What it did not yet have was the figure who would give it its soul. He arrived, the legends say, a few decades later — a blue-eyed monk from the west who would sit facing a wall until the wall, and Chinese Buddhism itself, were never the same. His name was Bodhidharma.
