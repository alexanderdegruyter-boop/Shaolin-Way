# The Warrior Monks

Shaolin's reputation for martial skill is not only legend — it rests on real events recorded in stone and in imperial histories. Two episodes, in particular, made the temple famous across China.

## The Thirteen Monks and the Tang Emperor

In the early **7th century**, as the Sui dynasty collapsed into civil war, Shaolin was drawn into the founding of the **Tang dynasty**. In **621 CE**, a group of Shaolin monks — remembered in tradition as the **thirteen staff-wielding monks** — aided the young general **Li Shimin** against the rival warlord **Wang Shichong**, helping to capture his nephew and secure a key fortress.

Li Shimin went on to become **Emperor Taizong**, one of the greatest rulers in Chinese history. He did not forget the monks' help. The temple was rewarded with land, grain, and imperial favour, and a stele erected at Shaolin still records the event. This is the historical kernel behind countless later stories of fighting monks: a moment when the monastery's martial ability shaped the fate of an empire.

With imperial patronage, Shaolin grew wealthy and large, and the practice of martial arts among its monks — to protect the temple's considerable property and to maintain discipline — became an established tradition.

## The Staff and the Pirates

Shaolin's second great martial chapter came nearly a thousand years later, during the **Ming dynasty**. In the mid-16th century, the southeast coast of China was ravaged by **wokou** — pirate raiders. The imperial army struggled, and on several occasions **Shaolin monks were recruited to fight**, most famously around the 1550s.

Ming-era writers praised the monks' skill, especially with the **staff (gun)**, which became the weapon most associated with Shaolin. A general of the period, Yu Dayou, is said to have visited the temple and found their staff method had decayed, taking two monks away to retrain — a story that, true or not, shows how seriously Shaolin's martial reputation was taken at the highest levels.

## Why Monks Fought

It can seem a paradox: Buddhist monks, sworn to non-violence, becoming famous as fighters. The Shaolin understanding softened the contradiction in several ways:

- Martial training was, first, a **discipline of body and mind** — health, focus, and self-mastery — not a hunger for combat.
- Fighting was framed as **protection**: of the temple, of the helpless, of order against banditry and invasion.
- The deepest skill was **restraint** — to be capable of force yet to govern it. *Martial virtue (wude)* held that the truly strong have no need to harm.

By the end of the Ming dynasty, "Shaolin" had become shorthand across China for martial excellence. The monastery stood at the height of its fame — a centre of Chan, of learning, and of the fighting arts. But the centuries to come would test it almost to destruction.
