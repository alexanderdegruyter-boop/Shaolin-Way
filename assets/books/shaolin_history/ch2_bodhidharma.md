# Bodhidharma and the Birth of Chan

No figure looms larger over Shaolin than **Bodhidharma** — known in Chinese as **Damo** (达摩) and in Japanese as *Daruma*. A Buddhist monk from India (or, some say, Persia), he is traditionally held to have arrived in China in the early **6th century CE** and to have made his way to Shaolin around 527 CE.

History and legend are tightly braided in his story, and it is worth keeping the two strands in mind even as we enjoy the whole cloth.

## Nine Years Facing a Wall

The most famous tale tells that Bodhidharma, finding the local Buddhism too concerned with ritual and scripture, withdrew to a cave above the monastery and sat in silent meditation, **facing a wall, for nine years**. So complete was his stillness that, the legend says, his shadow was burned into the rock.

From this radical emphasis on direct, wordless meditation grew **Chan Buddhism** — *Chan* being the Chinese rendering of the Sanskrit *dhyāna*, "meditation." Carried later to Japan, *Chan* became **Zen**. Bodhidharma is honoured as the **First Patriarch of Chan**, and Shaolin as the tradition's ancestral home. This is his enduring, genuine legacy: a school of Buddhism that points not to books but to the mind itself.

## "Before enlightenment, chop wood."

Chan's spirit is captured in sayings attributed to this lineage:

> "A special transmission outside the scriptures; not founded on words and letters. Pointing directly to the human mind, seeing one's nature and becoming Buddha."

For Chan, awakening is not a matter of accumulating knowledge but of clearing the mind to see what is already there. This plain, practical, here-and-now spirit is why Chan took such firm root among working monks who swept courtyards and worked fields — and why it pairs so naturally with the discipline of physical training.

## The Martial Legend

A later tradition — first written down many centuries afterward, and regarded by historians as legend rather than fact — credits Bodhidharma with teaching the monks a set of exercises to strengthen their bodies for long meditation. These are associated with two texts, the **Yijin Jing** ("Muscle-Tendon Change Classic") and the **Xisui Jing** ("Marrow-Washing Classic").

Whether or not Bodhidharma authored such exercises (the scholarly consensus is that the texts came much later), the story expresses a truth Shaolin lived by: **the body and the mind are cultivated together.** A monk who could not hold his posture could not hold his attention.

So we honour Bodhidharma twice over — as the historical father of Chan, and as the legendary patron of the idea that a strong body and a clear mind are one practice, not two.
